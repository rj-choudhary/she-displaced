01

The SDRS Formula

The She Displacement Risk Score (SDRS) is a weighted composite of four dimensions, each normalized to [0,1] relative to the global cohort for that year. Higher = more pressure toward women-specific climate displacement.

SDRS = 0.30 × n(Climate Vulnerability)
+ 0.20 × n(1 − Readiness)
+ 0.35 × n(1 − Gender Gap Score)
+ 0.15 × n(Disaster Burden)

Why these weights?

Gender inequality receives the largest weight (35%) — without it, SDRS would just be a tweaked ND-GAIN index. Climate vulnerability (30%) is the best-established signal in the literature. Readiness (20%) gets less weight because it partially overlaps with vulnerability in ND-GAIN's own construction. Disaster burden (15%) is intentionally smallest — one cyclone shouldn't spike a country to #1 without a real structural change.

Within-year normalization

Each component is min-max normalized across all countries within the same year before weighting. This means SDRS measures relative risk among peers that year — a country improving while others worsen still shows as high risk. Scores are comparable across years even when ND-GAIN or WEF shift their baselines.

Disaster burden compression

EM-DAT has extreme outliers (Pakistan 2022: 33 million affected). Raw values would let one event dominate. The log-compressed formula is:
D = 0.5×log(1+Affected) + 0.3×log(1+Deaths) + 0.2×log(1+Events)

Climate Vulnerability

Source: ND-GAIN Country Index

The vulnerability score from the Notre Dame Global Adaptation Initiative (ND-GAIN) measures a country's exposure, sensitivity, and adaptive capacity to climate change across six life-supporting sectors.

Why it's used as-is: The ND-GAIN vulnerability score is already a composite of these six sectors, normalized 0–1. Using it directly preserves the methodological integrity of a peer-reviewed index while keeping SDRS interpretable.
03

Adaptive Capacity Gap

Source: ND-GAIN Readiness Index

Readiness measures a country's ability to leverage investments and improvements for adaptation. It covers three sectors:

Sub-dimensionColumnWhat it measures
Economic Readinessreadiness_ecoBusiness environment; trade freedom; regulatory quality
Governance Readinessreadiness_govPolitical stability; rule of law; control of corruption
Social Readinessreadiness_socialEducation; ICT infrastructure; innovation capacity
Adaptive Capacity Gap = 1 − Readiness
Why inverted: A high readiness score means a country can adapt — so it reduces displacement risk. Inverting it converts readiness into a "gap" metric where higher = more vulnerable, consistent with the other components.
04

Gender Penalty

Source: WEF Global Gender Gap Report

The WEF Gender Gap Index measures parity between women and men across four sub-indices. A score of 1.0 = full parity; 0.0 = complete disparity.

Sub-indexColumnWhat it measures
Economic Participationecon_participationLabor force participation; wage equality; senior roles
Political Empowermentpolitical_empowermentWomen in parliament; ministerial positions; head of state
Educational Attainmenteducational_attainmentLiteracy rate; enrollment in primary, secondary, tertiary
Health & Survivalhealth_survivalSex ratio at birth; healthy life expectancy gap
Gender Penalty = 1 − Gender Gap Score
Why inverted: A high gender gap score means women are more equal — which reduces displacement risk. Inverting it creates a "penalty" metric: how much gender inequality is adding to risk. Countries with a gender penalty near 0.6 (like Afghanistan) have severe structural disadvantages that compound every climate event.
05

Gender Displacement Delta (GDD)

GDD = SDRS − n(Climate Vulnerability)

The GDD compares each country's composite SDRS against a "climate-only" counterfactual — what the index would say if physical climate exposure were the only driver. The sign tells the story: positive means gender, adaptive capacity, and disaster burden are pushing displacement risk above what climate exposure alone predicts; negative means climate is the dominant factor and social dimensions are not compounding it.

Policy interpretation: A country with GDD > +0.15 has its displacement risk substantially driven by gender inequality or weak adaptive capacity — the highest-leverage targets for social intervention. A country with GDD < 0 faces a risk profile dominated by its physical climate exposure, where mitigation and adaptation infrastructure matter most.

Data Vintage & Forward-Filling (2024–2025)

The SDRS spans 20 years (2006–2025). Not all source datasets publish at the same cadence:

ComponentLatest Ground-Truth2024–2025 Method
Climate Vulnerability (ND-GAIN)2023LOCF — 2023 values carried forward
Adaptive Capacity / Readiness (ND-GAIN)2023LOCF — 2023 values carried forward
Gender Gap Index (WEF)2025Observed — no imputation needed
Disaster Burden (EM-DAT)2025Observed — no imputation needed

LOCF (Last Observation Carried Forward) assumes that a country's climate vulnerability and readiness did not materially change between 2023 and 2025. This is a conservative assumption — structural indicators like infrastructure, governance, and ecosystem sensitivity shift slowly. The Gender Gap and Disaster Burden components use observed 2025 data, ensuring the SDRS still captures recent changes in gender policy and acute climate events.

Country Coverage

The dataset contains 187 country codes across all sources. Of these, 159 countries have sufficient data across all four SDRS components (ND-GAIN vulnerability, ND-GAIN readiness, WEF Gender Gap Index, EM-DAT disasters) to compute a complete SDRS score. The remaining 28 countries have partial coverage (typically missing Gender Gap Index data for small island states or microstates) and appear in component-specific charts but are excluded from the composite SDRS. The "159 countries scored" figure used throughout refers to countries with full SDRS coverage.

"High Risk" Threshold Definition

Throughout the dashboard, "high risk" refers to countries with SDRS ≥ 0.60 in 2025 — which corresponds to the top 20% (quintile) of all scored countries. As of 2025, 29 countries cross this threshold, representing the compounding-crisis set where both climate vulnerability and gender inequality are structurally deep. The 0.60 cutoff was chosen because it aligns with the quintile boundary and separates countries where the SDRS is driven by compounding stressors (multi-component elevation) versus single-component risk.

07

Limitations & Caveats

  • Weight sensitivity: The 30/20/35/15 weights are theoretically motivated but not empirically derived. The Innovation Lab (Chapter 6) allows users to test alternative weightings and observe how rankings shift.
  • Gender Gap Index coverage: The WEF index is only available from 2006, limiting the full SDRS to 2006–2025 (20 years). Vulnerability and readiness data extends back to 1995.
  • Forward-filling for ND-GAIN: Climate vulnerability and readiness scores for 2024–2025 use Last Observation Carried Forward (LOCF) from 2023. Structural indicators shift slowly, but this means the SDRS may understate rapid environmental degradation in those years.
  • Country-level aggregation: All scores are national averages. Sub-national variation — which is often where the most vulnerable women live — is not captured.
  • Correlation between components: Climate vulnerability and gender inequality are not independent — poorer countries tend to score worse on both. The SDRS does not attempt to decompose this correlation.
  • Displacement as outcome: The SDRS predicts risk of displacement, not actual displacement. Actual displacement data is fragmented and inconsistently reported globally.
